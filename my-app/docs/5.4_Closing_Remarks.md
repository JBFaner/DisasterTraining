# 13.4 Closing Remarks
*(Use **5.4** if your final manuscript follows Chapter V numbering instead of 13.x.)*

---

Completing **Local Government Unit 4: Disaster Preparedness Training and Simulation with AI Scenario Auto-Generation Using Gemini API** (AlertaraQC) did not feel like finishing a school requirement alone. For us—**Aquino, Brogada, Faner, Pastor, and Valdez**—it was months of turning classroom concepts into something Barangay San Agustin could actually use: a secure web platform where trainers can plan exercises, publish simulation events, track attendance, score performance, and issue certificates without relying on the same manual drill every year. When we started, the idea of connecting Laravel, React, MySQL, and the Gemini API into one LGU-ready workflow seemed larger than any single sprint. Looking back, that pressure is exactly what pushed us to work as a real development team rather than five individuals submitting separate pieces.

The work was not linear. We argued over backlog priorities, rebuilt flows after adviser feedback, and more than once stayed up debugging integration issues—from OTP login and audit logs to template-based simulation events and deployment on Hostinger. There were sprints where a feature looked “done” in code but failed in demo because we had not yet thought like LGU staff: *Approved Campaign → Exercise Plan → Template → Readiness → Publish → Monitoring*. Those moments were frustrating, but they taught us something lectures alone could not—requirements change when the user is a barangay trainer under time pressure, not a classmate testing a checkbox. We are grateful that our capstone adviser, **Ms. Richelyn A. Villasor**, and our panelists gave honest comments instead of easy approval; their questions during reviews forced us to defend our architecture, tighten our Scrum artifacts, and keep the pilot scope realistic for Barangay San Agustin, Novaliches, Quezon City.

We also owe much to the people who shared the problem with us before we ever wrote a line of code—the barangay and LGU stakeholders who explained how preparedness training is actually run on the ground, with limited staff, repeating scenarios, and the constant need to prove readiness without putting residents at risk. This project belongs to that conversation. If AlertaraQC helps even a small number of trainers run fresher, more challenging drills with AI-generated scenarios—and gives officials clearer records of who trained, how they scored, and what was certified—then the long hours in front of our screens will have meant something beyond a grade.

We do not present this system as a perfect or finished product. Some items remain for future work: wider LGU rollout, optional SMS alerts, deeper live-data integration, and longer pilot validation. Still, we leave this capstone confident that we delivered a working increment: a modular, documented, deployable platform aligned with our objectives and honest about its scope. To our families, friends, instructors, and to **Bestlink College of the Philippines**, thank you for the trust and patience. Above all, we thank God for the strength to see this through.

To the next batch of researchers and to the DRRM community: preparedness is not built in one drill, one semester, or one deployment. It is built through repeated practice, honest evaluation, and tools that respect how local government actually works. We hope AlertaraQC becomes one small part of that effort in Quezon City—and that whoever reads this document will treat it not only as an academic output, but as proof that student teams can contribute something useful when they listen to the community they serve.
