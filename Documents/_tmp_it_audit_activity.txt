PART B — MAJOR CASE STUDY
30 Points | 35 Minutes
THE UNIVERSITY THAT PASSED ITS AUDIT
Prime University received an “IT Controls Satisfactory” rating in its previous internal audit. Six months later, a new audit team begins a follow-up review.
CIO: “Nothing has changed since the previous audit. You can use the previous audit results.” The team verifies the claim.
EVIDENCE 1 — USER ACCOUNTS
1,850 active student accounts
420 faculty accounts
110 administrative accounts
14 system administrator accounts
6 accounts belong to employees who resigned.
3 administrator accounts have no documented owner.
2 faculty accounts have administrator-level access.
1 account has been inactive for 14 months but remains enabled.
IT administrator: “Those accounts are probably still needed.” No documentation supports the explanation.
EVIDENCE 2 — PASSWORD CONFIGURATION
Policy: minimum 12 characters
Policy: expiration every 90 days
Policy: MFA required for administrators
Actual: minimum 8 characters
Actual: expiration every 180 days
MFA enabled for 11 of 14 administrators
IT manager: “The difference is not a serious problem because employees still have passwords.”
EVIDENCE 3 — BACKUP
Backup jobs run nightly.
4 backup jobs failed in the last 30 days.
Nobody investigated the failures.
No restoration test has been documented for 10 months.
Most recent successful restoration test was 11 months ago.
Backup administrator: “We don't need to test restoration unless we lose data.”
EVIDENCE 4 — LOG MONITORING
Security officer says logs are checked regularly.
No log-review records were provided for the last three months.
Security officer: “We don't document every review because we're busy.”
EVIDENCE 5 — EMPLOYEE TERMINATION
HR provided the previous year's termination list.
6 former employees still have active accounts.
IT: “HR does not always inform us immediately.”
HR: “IT receives the termination notices through email.”
Neither department can provide a formal reconciliation report.

Question 3 – Evidence Reliability (5 points)
 
ANSWER:
Strongest to weakest:
1. System configuration
2. Backup logs
3. User account report
4. HR termination records
5. System log-review documentation
6. Security policy
7. Employee interview
8. IT manager verbal statement

Explanation:
System settings, logs, and account reports are stronger because they come from the system itself and can be checked again. HR records are useful but may be incomplete. A policy only shows what should happen, not what is really happening. Interviews and verbal statements are the weakest because people can be wrong, forget details, or try to defend their work without proof.
Rank strongest to weakest: Employee interview; System configuration; User account report; Security policy; Backup logs; IT manager verbal statement; HR termination records; System log-review documentation. Explain the ranking.
Question 4 – The Trap (5 points)
 
ANSWER (~270 words):
I disagree with the CIO. A previous audit that said “Satisfactory” only shows the situation at that time. Controls can get weaker after six months. People resign, settings change, and monitoring may stop. So the old result cannot automatically clear today’s findings.

Also, saying there was no known security breach does not mean controls are working. Some incidents are never reported, or the school simply has not discovered them yet. An auditor looks at whether controls are operating now, not only whether something bad already happened.

In this case, the evidence already shows real problems: former employees still have accounts, some admin accounts have no owner, password and MFA settings are weaker than policy, backups failed and were not investigated, and there are no log-review records. These are based on documents and system data, not guesses.

As an auditor, I should still report the findings if the evidence supports them. Management may not like the findings, but auditor judgment should follow the facts. The recommendation should explain the risk and what to fix. Ignoring findings because “nothing bad happened yet” is dangerous, especially for a university that holds student and payment information.
CIO: “The previous audit found no major problems, and we have not experienced a security breach. Therefore, there is insufficient justification for reporting these findings.”
Write 250–300 words discussing previous audit results, absence of known incidents, actual control operation, objective evidence, control testing, and auditor judgment.
Question 5 – Risk Prioritization (5 points)
 
ANSWER (1 = Most Critical, 6 = Least Critical):
1. Former employees with active accounts — easy to exploit if passwords are still known; high impact on student/email data
2. Problem admin accounts (no owner / excess rights / long unused) — high impact if misused
3. Password and MFA weaker than policy — makes accounts easier to break
4. Backup failures and no restore test — big problem if a disaster happens
5. Missing log-review evidence — harder to detect attacks early
6. Relying only on the previous audit rating — process issue that can hide other problems

I ranked them using likelihood, impact, how exposed the systems are, how sensitive the data is, how easy it is to abuse the weakness, and whether there are strong compensating controls (in this case, there are not).
Rank the six findings from 1 = Most Critical to 6 = Least Critical. Justify using likelihood, impact, exposure, sensitivity, ease of exploitation, and existing controls.
Question 6 – Management Response (5 points)
 
ANSWER — Two highest-risk findings:

Finding 1: Former employees still have active accounts
Risk: Unauthorized access to SIS, email, LMS, or payment-related data.
Recommended Action: Disable the 6 accounts now. Make a clear process so HR informs IT on the last day. Do weekly HR–IT account checks for the next 90 days.
Responsible Person/Department: IT Security / Account Management and HR.
Required Evidence of Completion: Disable logs, updated user list, signed reconciliation report.
Follow-up Test: Compare active accounts again with the latest HR resignation list; no resigned employee should still be active.

Finding 2: Admin accounts without proper control
Risk: Someone with high privilege can change systems or hide activity.
Recommended Action: Assign owners or disable unused/orphan admin accounts. Remove admin rights from faculty who should not have them. Require MFA for all admins and do monthly admin reviews.
Responsible Person/Department: IT Infrastructure / Network Admin, with CIO approval.
Required Evidence of Completion: Updated admin list with owners, change tickets, monthly review checklist.
Follow-up Test: Recheck the admin list for owners, unused accounts, and full MFA coverage.
For the two highest-risk findings, provide: Finding; Risk; Recommended Action; Responsible Person/Department; Required Evidence of Completion; Follow-up Test.
PART C — CASE SCENARIO
20 Points | 25 Minutes
SCENARIO 1 — THE AUDITOR CANNOT TRUST THE INTERVIEW
Network Administrator: “Yes. We check administrator accounts every month.”
When asked for monthly review records: “We don't keep records because this is a routine task.”
The account list shows 14 administrator accounts, 3 without identified owners, 2 belonging to former employees, and 1 unused for 9 months.
Administrator: “Those are probably old accounts. They aren't being used.”
Question 7 – Auditor Response (5 points)
 
ANSWER:
I should not just believe the interview. Next steps:
1. Treat the statement as a claim only, not as proof.
2. Get the full list of administrator accounts with last login and owner details.
3. Compare the list with HR active and resigned employees.
4. For the 3 accounts with no owner, 2 former employees, and 1 unused for 9 months, ask for written explanation or request that they be disabled.
5. Look for any tickets, emails, or checklists that show monthly reviews in the last 6 months.
6. If there is still no proof, document this as a finding with evidence.
7. Discuss with the auditee, then include it in the report.
Reporting comes after gathering enough evidence, not before.
What should the auditor do next? Explain the audit actions; do not simply say “Report it.”
Question 8 – Control Testing (5 points)
 
ANSWER — Three tests:

Test 1
Test Objective: Check if monthly admin account reviews really happen.
Procedure: Ask for review records for the last 6 months. If none are given, the control is not operating.
Evidence: Tickets, checklists, or emails.
Expected Result: Dated reviews that cover all admin accounts.

Test 2
Test Objective: Confirm resigned employees do not keep admin access.
Procedure: Match the admin list with the HR termination list.
Evidence: HR list and system account extract.
Expected Result: Zero resigned users with admin rights.

Test 3
Test Objective: Confirm unused admin accounts are disabled.
Procedure: Find accounts with no login for a long time (example: more than 90 days) and check if they are disabled.
Evidence: Last-login report.
Expected Result: Old unused accounts are disabled or formally approved to remain.
Design three tests using: Test Objective → Procedure → Evidence → Expected Result.
SCENARIO 2 — BACKUP ILLUSION
Backup administrator: “We have 100% backup compliance.” Dashboard: “Backup Job Completed: YES.”
However: the backup file is incomplete; no restoration test exists; storage has only 8% remaining capacity; two previous jobs failed; no incident ticket was created.
Question 9 – Backup Control Effectiveness (5 points)
 
ANSWER:
No. The auditor cannot conclude that the backup control is effective. A dashboard that only says “Backup Job Completed: YES” is not enough. The backup file is incomplete, there is no restoration test, storage is almost full (8% left), earlier jobs failed, and no incident ticket was made. Effective backup means the data can really be restored when needed. Right now, that is not proven.
Can the auditor conclude that the backup control is effective? Explain.
Question 10 – Evidence vs. Assumption (5 points)
 
ANSWER:
A. What the organization claims: They have 100% backup compliance and backups are complete.
B. What the evidence actually proves: There is a completion flag on the dashboard, some past jobs failed, storage space is very low, and there is no restore-test document.
C. What the evidence does NOT prove: That backup files are complete and usable, that recovery time goals can be met, that failed jobs were handled, or that important data can be restored.
D. What additional test is necessary: Do a controlled restore of an important system (example: sample SIS data) in a test environment, check if the restored files are complete, and review tickets/alerts for failed backups and low storage.
A. What the organization claims
B. What the evidence actually proves
C. What the evidence does NOT prove
D. What additional test is necessary
PART D — AUDIT FINDING CONSTRUCTION
15 Points | 20 Minutes
Question 11 – Prepare a Formal Audit Finding (15 points)
 
ANSWER — Chosen issue: Former employees with active accounts

CONDITION: Six accounts belonging to resigned employees are still enabled. HR and IT could not show a formal reconciliation report. IT said HR does not always inform them quickly, while HR said notices are sent by email, but there is no clear proof that access was always removed on time.

CRITERIA: Access should be removed when employment ends. HR and IT should compare resignation lists with active system accounts.

CAUSE: The process depends on informal email only. There is no clear deadline, no automatic disable step, and no regular reconciliation.

EFFECT/RISK: A former employee, or anyone who still knows the password, may still access student records, email, or related systems. This can lead to data leakage, unauthorized changes, and damage to the university’s reputation.

RECOMMENDATION: Disable the six accounts within 24 hours. Create a same-day disable process. Require an official ticket from HR for every resignation. Do weekly reconciliation for 90 days, then monthly. Report the results to the CIO.

Evidence Reference: User account extract (Evidence 1); HR termination list and interviews (Evidence 5)
Risk Rating: High
Management Response: Management may say they will disable the accounts and improve coordination between HR and IT.
Auditor Rebuttal: The plan is noted, but the risk remains until the accounts are disabled and a reconciliation report is shown and retested. A previous clean audit does not remove this current finding.
Choose ONE major issue and prepare:
CONDITION — What did you discover?
CRITERIA — What should have happened?
CAUSE — Why did it occur?
EFFECT/RISK — What could happen?
RECOMMENDATION — What should management do?
Also include: Evidence Reference; Risk Rating (Critical/High/Medium/Low); Management Response; Auditor Rebuttal.
PART E —  CRITICAL THINKING ESSAY
15 Points | 20 Minutes
Question 12 – When Compliance on Paper Is Not Compliance in Practice (15 points)
 
ANSWER (~650 words):
An auditor should still examine and test controls even if an organization already has policies, procedures, employees who say everything is fine, and a previous audit that was satisfactory. Paper compliance is not the same as real compliance. Policies only describe what should happen. They do not automatically prove that the control is working every day.

First is policy versus practice. A university may have a password policy that requires 12 characters, 90-day expiration, and MFA for administrators. But if the system is set to 8 characters, 180 days, and MFA is missing for some admins, then practice does not match the policy. If the auditor only reads the policy and believes management, the audit conclusion will be wrong. The same idea applies to backups. A written backup procedure means little if failed jobs are ignored and restore testing has not been done for almost a year.

Second is interview versus evidence. Interviews are useful because they give explanations and context. But interviews are weak if they stand alone. People may honestly believe a process is happening, or they may avoid admitting problems. In the case, the network administrator said monthly admin reviews are done, but there were no records. The security officer said logs are checked regularly, but also said reviews are not documented. When words and records do not match, the auditor should trust the evidence more than the interview.

This is why control testing is important. Control testing means the auditor checks how the control really operates. Examples are comparing system configuration with policy, matching resigned employees with active accounts, reviewing backup logs, and asking for restore-test documents. Without testing, the auditor is only collecting opinions.

Auditors should also use different types of evidence. Documentary evidence includes policies, tickets, and reports. Technical evidence includes system settings, account extracts, and logs. Observational evidence includes seeing physical controls like a locked server room. Interview evidence includes explanations from staff. All can be useful, but when they conflict, technical and documentary evidence are usually stronger.

Another reason to keep testing is that risk can exist even when no incident is known. The absence of a reported breach does not mean the school is safe. An unused admin account can sit quietly for months until someone abuses it. A backup may look successful on a dashboard until the day the school needs to restore and discovers the file is incomplete. Waiting for an incident before reporting a weakness is already too late.

Professional judgment also matters. Management may pressure the auditor to rely on the previous “Satisfactory” rating or to avoid findings because it looks bad. The auditor should stay independent. Findings should be based on evidence: what was found (condition), what should have happened (criteria), why it happened (cause), what could go wrong (effect), and what should be fixed (recommendation).

In short, policies, confident interviews, and old audit ratings are not enough. An auditor must still independently examine and test controls to know if they are really working. For a university handling student information and online payments, this careful approach is necessary to protect the school and its users.
